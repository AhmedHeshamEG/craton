# P10 — Listening: the ground's hum as an instrument

P10 comes in parts (D-137), the instruments before the model. This file grows one part at a time.

## P10a — The ear

### TLDR

Everything Craton has read so far arrives in bursts: a quake happens, a dot appears. Between
quakes the catalogue is silent, and most days are between quakes. But the ground is never silent.
Every seismometer records a constant hum, made mostly by ocean waves, and the rock it travels
through changes the hum a little when the rock changes.

P10a gives Craton an ear. It downloads the continuous recordings of every broadband station in a
region from any FDSN data centre (the standard every seismic network speaks), at one sample per
second, and turns each station-day into one token: how loud the ground hummed in three
frequency bands. A quake that shakes a few hours of the day doesn't move the number, because the
day's value is the *median* of its hours.

Around L'Aquila (seven stations, June 2008 to June 2010) that's 11,906 tokens. On the day of the
Mw 6.3 shock the fastest band jumps by about 20 dB: the aftershocks filled every hour, so even
the median heard them. Five days later it's back. The second instrument, how *fast* the hum
travels (dv/v), was tried on the same data: it drops across the shock, two to three times its
noise, once all nine component pairs are used. Cleaning it up is P10b.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Why the ground hums.** Ocean waves running into each other push on the sea floor twice per
wave period, and that pressure shakes the whole continent at periods of 3 to 10 seconds: the
*secondary microseism*. Waves breaking on the coast make a weaker hum at 10 to 20 s, the *primary
microseism*. Wind, rivers and traffic add the faster part. You can't feel any of it (it's
nanometres), but a broadband seismometer records it all day, every day, everywhere.

**2. Why a forecaster would want it.** Quakes are rare. A model that only reads quakes knows
nothing about the days without them. The hum is there every day, and two things about it can carry
information about the crust: how loud it is (P10a), and how fast it travels through the rock
(P10b, the velocity change). In the lab, the hum a fault makes predicts how close it is to
slipping; under Cascadia, the hum's power rose before slow-slip events. Whether any of that holds
for Apennine quakes is what P10c measures. It might be zero.

**3. One number per band, from a spectrum.** Take one hour of recording. A Fourier transform
splits it into how much shaking there was at each frequency: the *power spectrum*. Seismologists
report it as acceleration power, in decibels relative to 1 (m/s²)²/Hz, because that's how
Peterson's 1993 noise models (the textbook "quietest" and "loudest" Earth) are drawn: quiet sites
sit near −185 dB, a station by a road near −100. Average the spectrum over 0.05-0.1 Hz, 0.1-0.3 Hz
and 0.3-0.45 Hz and you have three numbers per hour.

**4. The median beats the quakes.** A day has 24 hours. If a quake shakes two of them, those two
hours are 50 dB louder, and the *mean* of the day jumps. The *median* barely moves: it's the
middle hour, and the middle hour was quiet. So the token says "how loud was the background",
which is the instrument we want; the quakes are already in the catalogue.

**5. Counts to metres.** A seismometer records *counts*. The station service publishes how many
counts one metre per second of ground velocity makes (the gain). Broadband sensors are flat over
these frequencies, so dividing by one number per channel is exact, give or take ~1 dB at the
slowest band on 40-second sensors. Velocity becomes acceleration by multiplying the power by
(2πf)².

**6. Why one sample per second.** Seismometers record at 100 samples per second. A day of that is
13.5 MB per component; at 1 sample per second it's 0.15 MB. Sixteen stations for three and a half
years is ~11 GB at 1 Hz and ~300 GB at 100 Hz. The hum we want lives below 0.5 Hz anyway, which
is exactly what 1 Hz can see (Nyquist: half the sample rate).

### The LLM analogy

A new *modality*. Craton was a text model reading one kind of token (quakes, then GPS, then
tides). Adding the hum is like adding audio to a text model: a different stream with its own
rhythm (one token per day, never silent), its own preprocessing (a spectrum instead of a
tokenizer), and soon its own encoder (P10c). The sensible order is the same as in multimodal
LLMs: make the new data clean and well described first, then teach the model to read it.

Where the analogy breaks: an audio model hears speech meant to carry information. The hum
carries information about the crust only by accident, through tiny changes in a signal made by
the ocean. Most of what's in it is weather at sea.

### What the code does

- `configs/regions/central_italy_noise.yaml`, `laquila_noise.yaml`: which box, which years,
  which data centre, which networks, which channel. Change these and the same code listens to
  California (EarthScope) or New Zealand (GeoNet).
- `craton/adapters/waveforms.py`: asks the station service which stations in the box have a
  1 Hz channel, then downloads one station-month per request, four at a time, into the raw store.
  A month the centre has nothing for is stored empty, so it isn't asked again.
- `craton/adapters/noise.py`: decodes miniSEED (`pymseed`), lays each day on a 1 Hz grid with
  gaps left empty, keeps the complete hours, and on the GPU detrends, tapers, FFTs and averages
  the bands; the day is the median of its hours.
- `craton/spec/instruments.py`: the new instrument `seismic_noise_power` (Token Spec 1.4.0),
  three channels in dB, qualifiers station, component, channel.

### The numbers

**What came down.** Around L'Aquila: seven stations with a 1 Hz channel, 152 station-months,
2.1 GB, in about 45 minutes from INGV. Central Italy, around the 2016 sequence: fourteen stations,
650 station-months asked for (443 with data), 6.1 GB, in about two and a half hours.

**What became tokens.** L'Aquila: 11,906 station-day-components. The 4,876 left out are honest
gaps: 1,579 days with fewer than 8 complete hours, 145 days outside any channel epoch, 3 days
mixing sample rates, plus 3,149 damaged records (all in June-September 2008, at three stations)
that the decoder drops one at a time instead of losing the month. Central Italy: 33,572 tokens;
6,162 days with too few hours, and 551 days rejected because they were quieter than the quietest
Earth (Peterson's low noise model, minus 10 dB) in some band. Those are dead channels: NRCA after
18 January 2017, long stretches at TERO. Without the rule they'd read as the calmest days on
record, and a model would learn from them.

**Does it hear anything?** The ocean band (0.1-0.3 Hz) sits at −133 to −135 dB on quiet days,
between Peterson's quiet and loud Earth, and moves ~10 dB with storms at sea. The short band
(0.3-0.45 Hz) at FAGN and AQU:

| day | −2 | −1 | 6 Apr 2009 | +1 | +2 | +5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FAGN, dB | −140 | −137 | **−118** | −129 | −128 | −135 |
| AQU, dB | −141 | −139 | **−115** | −130 | −131 | −133 |

That's the aftershock sequence, heard through the median. And yet it isn't the loudest day of
the two years: 14 January 2009 was about as loud at every station at once, with no quake at all.
Italy's road agency reported violent storms, wind and heavy swell the day before, so it's most
likely weather. Central Italy says the same thing more loudly. Amatrice (24 August 2016, 01:36 UTC) barely moves
the median. The catalogue lists ten thousand aftershocks a day after it, but the big ones fill a few
hours and the small ones make almost no sound below 0.5 Hz. The two loudest days that week were 22
and 23 August, a steady +18 dB at every station, on days with fewer quakes than usual and none
above M2. After Visso and Norcia
(26 and 30 October) the aftershocks did fill the days, and the short band sits 15-20 dB up for a
week. The loudest day of all three and a half years is 30 October 2018 at OFFI, during Storm
Vaia. The hum is loud for many reasons, and a forecaster has to learn which ones
matter; that's why P10c measures it instead of assuming it. Nothing stood out before the shock
in these two weeks; whether anything does over months is P10c's question, against the
catalogue that already knows about the aftershocks.

**The speed of the hum, first try.** Correlating the hum between stations gives a wave that
travels between them, and the late part of that wave (the coda) arrives a little later when the
rock slows down. With 1 Hz data, 0.1-0.45 Hz, and stacks that look only backwards:

| method | 10-day stack | 50-day stack |
|---|---|---|
| one station, its own components | 0.25-0.42% scatter | |
| one pair of stations, vertical only | 0.52% | 0.29% |
| all pairs averaged, vertical only | 0.17% | 0.09% |
| all pairs averaged, all nine component pairs | 0.035% | 0.020% |

Nine components is the trick: correlating every direction at one station with every direction at
the other gives nine views of the same rock, and their noise doesn't line up. Measured honestly
(on days outside the reference), the nine-component 10-day series scatters by 0.06%, and across
the shock it drops from +0.07% to −0.11% and stays there for ten days: two to three times the
scatter. A year later it wanders to −0.2% with no quake at all, which is the seasons (rain,
groundwater, ocean storms). So the second instrument is real but not finished: P10b fits the
seasons out before it's allowed near a forecast (D-138). The published L'Aquila drop is
strongest at 0.5-1 Hz, above what one sample per second can see, which is the fallback if 1 Hz
isn't enough.

<details><summary>Why does a storm in the Atlantic show up on a seismometer in Umbria?</summary>
Ocean waves press on the sea floor, and that pressure shakes the crust at 3-10 s periods (the
secondary microseism). Those waves travel thousands of kilometres through the continent, so a big
storm is "heard" across Europe.</details>

<details><summary>A quake shook two hours of the day. What happens to the token?</summary>
Almost nothing: the day's value is the median of its hourly powers, and the median is set by the
quiet hours. The quake is in the catalogue already.</details>

<details><summary>Why not record at 100 samples per second?</summary>
30 times the disk and download time, and the hum the instruments need is below 0.5 Hz, which
1 sample per second already captures.</details>

<details><summary>Why is a level jump on the day a sensor was replaced not a discovery?</summary>
A new sensor has a new gain and sits differently in its vault. The data card says it: a step
across a sensor swap is the sensor, not the Earth.</details>

### Glossary

- **Microseism**: the ground's constant hum from ocean waves, strongest at 3-10 s periods.
- **Power spectrum (PSD)**: how much shaking there is at each frequency.
- **dB re 1 (m/s²)²/Hz**: ten times the log of acceleration power; +10 dB is ten times the power.
- **FDSN**: the federation whose web services every seismic network uses, so one adapter reads
  them all.
- **miniSEED**: the compressed format continuous seismic data is stored in.
- **Nyquist frequency**: half the sample rate, the fastest wiggle a recording can see.

## P10b — How fast the hum travels

### TLDR

Two seismometers 30 km apart hear the same hum. Line up a day of one with a day of the other and
look for the delay at which they match best: that delay is how long a wave takes to go from one
to the other. Do it with the whole messy tail of the match (the *coda*, waves that bounced around
the crust for a minute) and you get a very sensitive speedometer. When the rock cracks or gets
wetter, waves slow down by a fraction of a percent, and every late wiggle arrives a little later.

P10b builds that speedometer for every pair of nearby stations, every day, as a new token:
**dv/v**, the relative change in speed against a quiet reference period. It's clean: averaged over
central Italy's 41 pairs it wobbles by 0.04% from one ten-day window to the next, and eighteen
months after the 2016 sequence it's back at zero.

And it doesn't see the quakes. Amatrice is a small dip (twice the wobble). Norcia, the biggest
shock in Italy in 36 years, makes it go *up*, at one sample per second and again at twenty.
A new benchmark, CB-28, asks the plainest question you can ask of a station network: hide one
station, can the others rebuild its numbers? Right now the best answer is "assume nothing
changed". The neighbours' numbers make it worse.

### The idea, step by step

1. **Correlate the hum.** Take an hour from station A and the same hour from station B. Squash
   each to its sign (+1 or −1), so a passing truck or a quake counts as much as a quiet minute, and
   flatten its spectrum inside 0.1-0.45 Hz. Slide one against the other and record how well they
   match at every delay from −120 to +120 s. Average the day's 24 hours.
2. **All nine directions.** Each station has three sensors (up, north, east), so a pair has nine
   combinations. Each is a separate look at the same rock.
3. **Reference.** Average a year of quiet days: that's what the match *should* look like.
4. **Stretch.** For today (in fact the last 10 or 30 days, stacked, because one day is too
   noisy), stretch the reference in time by a tiny factor until it matches best. Stretched by
   +0.1% means today's waves arrive 0.1% later: the rock is 0.1% slower.
5. **Combine.** Average the nine, trusting each by how well its stretch matched.
6. **Clean.** Ocean storms change what the hum is made of, and that alone can fake a change in
   speed. So on the quiet reference days only, fit how dv/v follows the hum's loudness and its
   tilt between frequency bands (and the season, if the reference holds a year), then subtract
   that fit everywhere. Which of those to use is decided inside the reference too: fit on its first
   three quarters, check on the last.

### The LLM analogy

The correlation is self-attention between two sensors with one query: "at which delay do you
look like me?" The reference is a frozen embedding of normal. dv/v is the drift of today's
embedding from it along one direction (time stretch). The correction is the same idea as
removing a known confound before reading a probe.

### What the code does

- `craton/adapters/interferometry.py`: per month, decode every station, whiten all channel-hours
  in one GPU call, correlate every pair within 80 km on nine components, keep the day's mean over
  the hours both sides recorded whole. Cached under `data/cache/velocity/`, keyed by the raw files.
- `craton/adapters/velocity.py`: the math (whitening, correlation, stretching on the GPU, Weaver
  et al.'s error, trailing stacks, the per-pair series, the correction fit).
- `craton/adapters/dvv.py`: the adapter. Reads the cached correlations and P10a's noise tokens,
  chooses the correction terms on the reference, writes one token per pair, day and stack.
- `craton/bench/velocity.py`: CB-28 and its two floors.

### The numbers

| | central Italy | L'Aquila |
|---|---|---|
| pairs with a quiet reference | 41 of 86 | 9 of 20 |
| tokens (two stacks) | 81,264 | 10,146 |
| network scatter, 10 days, held out | 0.039% | 0.085% |
| one pair's scatter | ~0.24% | ~0.15% |
| correction chosen | none | loudness + tilt |
| across the shock | Amatrice −0.08%, Norcia +0.07% | −0.11% |

CB-28, RMSE of the hidden pairs' dv/v after the reference (lower is better):

| floor | score | central Italy | L'Aquila |
|---|---|---|---|
| `velocity_zero`, "nothing changed" | **0.347%** | 0.293 | 0.401 |
| `velocity_neighbours`, the nearby pairs that day | 0.378% | 0.302 | 0.455 |

The 20 Hz check, four stations around Norcia, 0.5-1 Hz, one component: scatter 0.13%, Amatrice
−0.16%, Norcia +0.23%. Same story, noisier.

Why Norcia goes up is the open question. The likeliest answer: thousands of aftershocks filled the
hours being correlated, all from one direction, and changed the shape of the coda in a way the
stretch reads as faster rock. Published work on this sequence, with more stations and up to 1 Hz,
did see drops. Getting there means choosing hours with the aftershocks in mind, not tuning the
window until 2016 says yes.

This phase only proves things on central Italy. The regions are YAML files; another network
needs its own file with a quiet reference, nothing else.

### Check yourself

<details><summary>Why squash the recordings to +1 and −1 before correlating?</summary>
A quake or a storm is thousands of times louder than the quiet hum. Without the squash, one loud
hour would decide the whole day's correlation; with it, every minute votes once.</details>

<details><summary>Why does a slower crust stretch the coda instead of shifting it?</summary>
A wave that travelled for 40 s is 0.1% late by 0.04 s; one that travelled for 10 s, by 0.01 s.
The delay grows with travel time, so the late part moves more than the early part: a stretch.</details>

<details><summary>Why can't the station installed the day after L'Aquila give a dv/v?</summary>
dv/v is a change against a quiet reference. A station that only ever heard the aftershock
sequence has no quiet days to compare with.</details>

<details><summary>Why is "nothing changed" a harder floor to beat than "average the neighbours"?</summary>
Because here the neighbours' numbers are mostly their own noise. Averaging noise into a guess adds
error; guessing zero adds none. A model only wins CB-28 if it finds the part of dv/v the paths
really share.</details>

### Glossary

- **Coda**: the long tail of a seismogram, waves scattered around the crust before arriving.
- **dv/v**: relative change in seismic speed; −0.1% means waves are 0.1% slower than the reference.
- **Stretching method**: find the time stretch that best maps the reference coda onto today's.
- **Stack**: the average of several days' correlations, to beat the noise.
- **Whitening**: flattening a spectrum so every frequency in the band weighs the same.
