# P9 — The hidden state: stress, pressure and the wiring

P9 comes in parts (D-106; nine since D-116), the tests first. This file grows one part at a time.

## P9a — Which way the crust is squeezed

### TLDR

Every forecast Craton has made so far is graded on quakes: did it give the real ones a high
probability. P9 wants more. If the model has really understood the crust, it should know things
about it nobody showed it, and the first of those is the **stress**: which way the rock is being
squeezed.

Nobody measures stress at depth directly, but its *direction* has been read in tens of thousands
of places: from how quakes slipped, from how boreholes break, from cracks pumped open with water.
The World Stress Map collects all of it (100,842 readings, free, CC BY 4.0).

CB-23 turns that into an exam. In 983 half-degree cells across seven regions, give the direction
of largest horizontal squeeze, reading **only** where, when and how big the quakes were. Never how
they slipped. Score: the angle you're off by, 0° to 90°. A coin gets 45°, and needs no GPU.

The floors: the long axis of the quake cloud gets **44.7°** (no better than the coin), one
direction per region **26.7°**, and copying the neighbouring cells' answers **16.6°**. Craton has
no answer yet. That comes in P9c.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Stress has a direction.** Push a book from both ends and it bends; push the crust from two
sides and it breaks along faults. At any point there's one horizontal direction in which the
squeeze is strongest, called **SHmax**. In central Italy it runs north-west to south-east, along
the Apennines: the range is being pulled apart sideways (north-east to south-west), and the
squeeze is what's left along it.

**2. Direction only, and it's axial.** SHmax at 10° and at 190° is the same line, a squeeze has
no front and back. So angles are compared on a half circle: the most you can be wrong is 90°. To
average directions like these, double every angle first, average the points on the circle, halve
the result. That's the "axial mean"; it's why 175° and 5° average to 0°, not 90°.

**3. How the truth is read.** Most readings (85%) are **focal mechanisms**: from the pattern of
first motions a quake sends to the stations (push or pull), seismologists work out how the fault
slipped, and from that, roughly, the squeeze. One mechanism gives the direction to ±25° at best
(quality C). Borehole breakouts (the hole goes oval along the weak direction) and hydraulic
fractures (a crack opens across the weakest push) are better, A or B. CB-23 keeps A to C,
weights each by how good it is, and scores cells that hold three readings or more.

**4. Why a quake catalogue might know.** The catalogue Craton reads has no mechanisms in it, only
dots. But dots aren't random: quakes line up along faults, and faults strike in directions the
stress chooses. Aftershocks crowd where a big quake loaded the rock and stay away from where it
unloaded it, a pattern that depends on how the fault was squeezed. A model that has really
learned "where the next quakes go" may have learned the squeeze on the way. CB-23 checks.

**5. The floors, and what they tell.** Three simple methods, each a different kind of knowledge:

- *Quake lineation* reads exactly what Craton reads: within 50 km of a cell, the long axis of the
  cloud of epicentres, taken as SHmax. It scores 44.7°, a coin. Split by the kind of faulting it
  makes the point: 38° on normal faults (where the fault does run along SHmax), 47° on strike-slip
  (where SHmax is ~30-45° off the fault), 50° on thrusts (where it's across). A clean fault line
  would give ~0°, ~35° and ~90°; at this scale the cloud's axis is mostly noise. Reading the
  squeeze off the dots takes more than drawing a line through them.
- *Regional average*: one direction per region, the mean of every other cell's answer. 26.7°.
  Fine where a whole region is pushed one way (California 16°), poor where it turns (Japan 35°,
  Turkey 38°).
- *Interpolation*: the other cells' answers, weighted by distance. 16.6°. This is the honest top:
  it reads the truth next door. Beating it from quakes alone would be a real finding.

**6. Never let it leak.** The World Stress Map never becomes tokens. It's a *truth source*,
outside the token store, so no region file can list it and no training run can see it. The two
floors that read answers are handed every cell's answer except the one they're asked about.

### The LLM analogy

**Probing.** Train a language model on next-word prediction, then ask whether its insides know
things nobody labelled: the part of speech of a word, the syntax tree of a sentence. If a small
probe can read them off, the model learned them on the way. CB-23 is a probe for geology: train
on "where do the next quakes go", ask for the stress. The difference is that here the probe's
answer key comes from a different instrument entirely.

- `craton/bench/stress.py`: the cells, the axial maths, the `StressMapper` protocol, CB-23 and its

## P9b — Where the sequence goes next, and the physics floor

### TLDR

Two new yardsticks, still no new model.

**CB-24, migration.** In 2016 central Italy didn't shake in one place: Amatrice in August,
Visso and Norcia 25-30 km north in October, Campotosto to the south in January. CB-24 cuts
that into 0.1° cells and asks, before every new cell lights up (its first M3+ quake), which
dark cell is next. You may read the decades before, and the cells lit so far. Nothing else.
Sixteen sequences, from central Italy to Alaska. A coin gets the next cell into its top 3 about
0.3% of the time. A ruler (the cells nearest the last one that lit) gets 9.3%. That's the bar
for the wiring Craton learns in P9d.

**The CRS floor.** The physics version of "quakes make quakes": every quake shifts the stress
around it, and the faults answer on rate-and-state friction's clock. On CB-02 it scores −7.52
bits per quake. ETAS gets −7.118. Physics with a ruler-and-catalogue fault model loses to
statistics, by 0.40 bits, the same story Mancini et al. told in 2019 until they fed it
published slip models.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Sequences migrate.** A big quake isn't the end of a story; it's a push. The rock next to
the rupture gets loaded, sometimes over its limit, and months later the next patch goes. Where
it goes depends on how the faults are connected, which nobody maps completely.

**2. Make "where next" a test.** Divide the box into 0.1° cells (~10 km). A cell *lights* at its
first quake of the scored size after the first big shock. Before each lighting, rank every dark
cell. If the one that lit is in your top 3, that's a hit. Average over the first 50 lightings
of a sequence, then over sixteen sequences.

**3. Why only the lightings?** If you could see every aftershock, the question turns into "where
are aftershocks piling up right now", which ETAS answers well without knowing any wiring.
Showing only the lightings forces the question CB-24 is about: given *where* the sequence has
been, where does it jump?

**4. The ruler.** Rank cells by distance from the one that lit last. Sequences creep, so this is
hard to beat. In central Italy it hits CI_9.3% of the time; at Ridgecrest 29%.

**5. Coulomb stress, in one picture.** A normal fault slips: the block above drops. The rock off
the fault's two ends gets stretched in the same sense, so faults there that point the same way
are pushed towards failing (stress up). The rock off its sides, in the block that dropped, is
relaxed (stress down, a "shadow"). The number is ΔCFF = Δτ + μ′Δσₙ: the extra shear in the slip
direction, plus friction times how much the fault was unclamped.

**6. Rate-and-state, the clock.** Dieterich (1994) turned lab friction into a law for how a
population of faults answers a stress step. Step up by ΔCFF and the rate jumps by about
e^(ΔCFF/Aσ), then fades back over a time tₐ, just like Omori's law. Step down and the rate drops
and recovers just as slowly. Aσ (how sensitive the faults are) and tₐ (how long they remember)
are the only two numbers the floor fits.

**7. The floor, built from what any catalogue has.** Every M3+ quake is a stress source: the big
ones (M5.4+) as rectangles of the size Wells & Coppersmith give a magnitude, the small ones as a
blob that fades as 1/r³. The faults that fail are the region's typical ones (central Italy:
NW-SE normal faults dipping SW). Background from ETAS's own map, so the only difference between
the two floors is the physics of triggering.

### The LLM analogy

**A hand-written prior versus a learned one.** CRS is like a rule-based grammar: every rule comes
from theory and you can read it. ETAS is like an n-gram model: no theory of why, just measured
statistics of what follows what. For a while the rules lose to the statistics, because the
rules need inputs (fault slip, fault geometry) you rarely have. CB-24 then asks the
representation question: does a model's internal "attention" between places match where things
actually go next?

### What the code does

- `craton/bench/migration.py`: lightings, the `MigrationRanker` protocol (score every cell, the
  harness asks once per lighting), CB-24 and its two floors.
- `craton/bench/crs.py` + `configs/floors/crs.yaml`: geometry (strike, dip, rake → vectors), the
  rectangle and the 1/r³ source, ΔCFF through `cutde`, Dieterich's state bank in log space so a
  deep shadow never overflows, the fit and the forecast in one pass over time.
- `scripts/figures/migration.py`: the order central Italy lit up.

   *(Up: that's where the next segment tends to go. The shadow is off its sides.)*

## P9c — A hidden stress state, and why it didn't pay

### TLDR

You gave the Field two new parts, each a piece of physics with a dial that starts at zero.
The **clock** lets each cell's memory run faster or slower, the way a fault loaded faster
plays its aftershocks out faster. The **steer** lets each cell stretch the patch of past quakes
it looks at along a direction it picks. At zero both are the old Field exactly. Trained, both
made CB-02 worse on every seed (clock by 0.04 to 0.08 bits, steer by 0.01 to 0.03), so neither
was kept. The steer's direction, read as the crust's squeeze, misses by 43° on CB-23: it
learned which way the quakes line up, not which way the rock is pushed.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Seismicity as a stress meter.** Dieterich's law says an aftershock sequence lasts
tₐ = Aσ/τ̇: the faster the crust is loaded (τ̇), the shorter the sequence. So how fast
quakes die out in a place tells you about the stress there. That's the hidden state you were
after: something you can't see, read off something you can.

**2. Time running faster is a slide.** The memory keeps nine counters per cell that fade with
lifetimes from an hour to twelve years, each four times longer than the last. Make time run
4× faster and every counter acts like the next shorter one. So "how fast time runs here" is
one number s that slides the bank along by s steps (with blending in between). s = 0 is the
old memory. This is the SITHCon trick: rescaling time is shifting an index.

**3. A stretched kernel is a recipe.** The memory blurs past quakes with a round Gaussian.
Stretch it along an angle θ, g(r)·(1 + e·cos 2(φ − θ)), and the sum over quakes splits into
the round sum I and one complex number Q = Σ g(r)·e^{2iφ}, the **quadrupole moment**. So you
compute I and Q once and can stretch any way later: log I + log(1 + Re(w̄·q)), q = Q/I.

**4. Make the angle mean something.** The head that picks w only sees things that don't change
when you rotate the map (the cell's own counters and |q|), and multiplies q, which does
rotate. Rotate every quake by α and the chosen angle rotates by α, exactly. The head has no
idea where north is, so any angle it picks must come from the quakes themselves. That's what
lets you grade it against the World Stress Map with no head trained for it.

**5. Start as the physics.** Both heads' last layers start at zero, so the untrained net passes
the memory through bit for bit. Whatever the ablation measures is what the block learned.

**6. The result.** Both blocks lost. A plausible reading: the Field already reads all nine
lifetimes at once and compares neighbouring cells with attention, so the new dials only
re-express things it could already do, and the extra freedom costs a little on the test
year. And the angle followed the lines the quakes draw. In the Apennines faults are normal
and the squeeze runs along them, so the angle lands near the truth (31° to 33° off there,
about what lineation alone gets). On thrust and strike-slip faults it doesn't.

### The LLM analogy

**The clock is Mamba's selective decay**: a state-space model whose forgetting rate depends on
the input, here forced to mean one physical thing. **The steer is an equivariant layer**,
like the networks for molecules that rotate their outputs with their inputs. And the result is
the familiar one from probing: a direction the model found useful isn't necessarily the
direction you hoped it would find. A probe that reads "the angle" reads whatever made the loss
go down.

### What the code does

- `craton/field/steer.py`: the round and quadrupole kernels in km, convolved over every memory
  counter on the GPU, giving q per cell, day and counter.
- `craton/field/hidden.py`: `shift_bank` (the slide), `HiddenFieldNet` (the two heads in front of
  the P6 Field), `make_net` (the plain Field when no `hidden:` section is set).
- `craton/field/stressmap.py`: lays 0.1° cells over a CB-23 region, runs the steer head of the
  Field trained for CB-02, and sums its stretch into each 0.5° cell, weighted by where the
  quakes are.

## P9d — Who triggers whom, what the network saw, and where understanding has to come from

### TLDR

You built two more pieces of physics in front of the Field. The **wiring** keeps ETAS's count
of aftershocks for every quake and learns only where they land. The **camera** works out from
the catalogue alone how small a quake the network could see at each moment, and counts the
quakes it must have missed. Both made CB-02 worse on every seed (wiring by about 0.01 bits,
camera by 0.03 to 0.09), so neither was kept. The new test CB-24 shows something more
important: the Field knows better than ETAS where a sequence goes next, but only in Italy.
Outside it, it is ETAS to the last digit. A model that understood quakes would not stop at a
border, so the rest of P9 is rebuilt around three tests of understanding.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Split ETAS by who sent what.** ETAS's rate in a cell is a background plus a sum over every
past quake. Group that sum by the cell the parent sat in and its magnitude band, and you get a
table: source cell, target cell, how many offspring. That table is the wiring ETAS assumes,
round kernels everywhere.

**2. Let attention move offspring, never create them.** Each source cell gets a query, each
target a key, and their dot product tilts the kernel toward some targets. Then each row is
rescaled so the source still sends exactly ETAS's number of offspring. You can't buy
likelihood by inventing aftershocks; you can only say where they go. The query starts at
zero, so the untrained wiring is ETAS's round kernel, exactly.

**3. Why conservation mattered.** The first version let the attention add and remove rate
freely, with a learned "sink" for the background. The Field's own correction and the
attention then pushed in opposite directions and cancelled each other: big weights inside,
ETAS outside. A conservation law took that freedom away.

**4. The eyes.** A network catches every quake above its completeness magnitude mc and only
some below. Make a histogram of the last 200 quakes' magnitudes: it rises as the quakes get
smaller (Gutenberg-Richter) until the network starts missing them, then falls. The peak, plus
0.2, is mc. Computed before each moment, never after, so it is honest in a forecast.

**5. The world.** Above mc the network saw everything, so those quakes are a fair sample of the
sizes. With b = 1, every quake above M2 stands for ten above M1. So each quake above mc counts
10^(b·(mc − floor)) and the ones between the floor and mc count zero, because they are already
inside that weight. Survey statisticians call this Horvitz-Thompson: weight each sample by one
over its chance of being seen.

**6. Why the camera lost.** CB-02 is graded by INGV's own catalogue, blind spots and all. The
old Field learned that blindness directly, and on a test that is graded by the same blindness,
that is the right bet. The camera forecasts the world and then has to push it back through
estimated eyes, which lag behind the real ones for hours after a mainshock. Correct physics,
wrong test for it.

**7. The real finding: CB-24.** As a ranker of the next cell to light up, the Field beats ETAS
in Amatrice and L'Aquila (0.194 against 0.174) and ties it exactly on the eight sequences
abroad. Five blocks on the same catalogue (clock, steer, wiring, camera and P8f's
curriculum) all lost; the one input that ever paid on every seed was new data, GPS.

### The three tests of understanding

What would convince you a model understands quakes, rather than knowing Italy?

- **It works where it never trained.** Feed it numbers without units (counts over what ETAS
  expects, magnitudes above the local mc, distances in rupture lengths) so an M6 in Japan
  looks like an M6 in Italy, train on every region but one, score on the one left out.
- **Its inside is physical.** Force every forecast through Dieterich's rate-and-state law, so
  the only thing the network may output is a stress history. Then check that stress against
  a truth it never saw: a simulated planet where you planted it, and Groningen, where the gas
  company's reservoir model gives the pressure drop on a 500 m grid every year since 1957.
- **What it learned fits in a formula.** Run symbolic regression on what the network adds to
  ETAS. If ten terms keep most of the gain on years it never saw, the gain is a rule, and a
  rule can't be memorised.

### The LLM analogy

**The wiring is attention with a conservation law**: like a softmax, each row sums to a fixed
budget, but here the budget is a physical count. **The camera is the gap between a language
model's training data and the language**: a model trained on one source learns that source's
habits, and a test drawn from the same source rewards it for doing so. And CB-24 is the
familiar lesson of benchmarks: a score on the distribution you trained on says little about
the one you didn't.

### What the code does

- `craton/field/wiring.py`: `sources` splits ETAS's triggered rate by source cell and band;
  `WiredFieldNet` is the conserving attention; `pair_matrix` gives who-triggered-whom per day.
- `craton/field/camera.py`: `completeness_before` (the eyes, on the GPU), `world_weights`,
  `band_seen`.
- `craton/field/migrate.py`: any Field, or ETAS, as a CB-24 ranker, zero-shot outside Italy.
- `KernelCache` in `craton/field/base.py`: each quake's kernel row is computed once and reused
  by later queries, which brought a CB-24 query from 26 s to 2 s.

## P9e — Did it cheat, and what did it learn?

### TLDR

Two questions before believing the Field's gain over ETAS. **Did it cheat?** Three checks say no:
fake quakes after the cut change nothing it trains on; trained as if the data ended a year earlier,
it gains nearly the same; trained with every day's answers swapped onto another day, it loses everything.
**What did it learn?** Symbolic regression squeezed it into formulas of one to four terms that keep
84% of its gain on years they never saw (CB-26). The shortest: raise ETAS where a place's last 3 years
outweigh its last 12. The humbling part: one constant factor on ETAS already keeps 61%, so most of
the gain is "more quakes than ETAS expects" and the rule is the rest.

### The idea, step by step

**1. What a leak looks like.** A forecast for 25 August 2016 may read every quake before that
midnight and nothing after. If even one later quake sneaks into training, the model can score well
by remembering instead of forecasting. Most leaks are bugs: an off-by-one at the cut, a window that
includes its own end.

**2. Canaries.** Miners took a canary underground because it dies first. Here the canaries are fake
quakes: a swarm and an M6.5 placed exactly at and after the cut. Build every training array twice,
with and without them. If a single number differs, something reads past the cut. The test also
checks the opposite: one fake quake two days *before* the cut must change the last day, or the
check would pass by being blind.

**3. Move the cut.** Train as if the data stopped a year earlier, fit ETAS to that earlier data too,
and score on the same 2016-17 test. A model that had quietly used the months before Amatrice would
lose its edge. This one gains +0.30 and +0.26 on two seeds, against +0.30 and +0.29 as trained.

**4. Shuffle time.** Keep every day's memory where it was, but hand it another day's quakes. If the
gain came from something that holds on every day (a fixed map of where ETAS is off), the shuffled
model would keep it. It doesn't: it falls to −1.4, far below ETAS. With time broken, the busiest
memories (right after a mainshock) get matched with ordinary quiet days, so the network learns to
squash ETAS's aftershock peaks, exactly the wrong lesson. The gain is about *when*.

**5. Squeeze it into a formula.** The Field reads 54 counters per cell (quakes fading over an hour
to 12 years, in the cell or nearby, plain or weighted by size) and outputs a correction c to ETAS's
rate. Symbolic regression (PySR) is an evolutionary search over formulas: it breeds expressions
like `0.13 · p_16d_k10` and keeps the ones that match c best at each length. You fit the formula
to the *network*, not to the quakes, so the formula is a reading of what the network does.

**6. Fit where the bits are.** Most cell-days are quiet; a mistake there costs almost nothing. For
a Poisson count with rate λ, answering f where the network says c costs about ½λ(f − c)². So rows
are drawn in proportion to λ and fitted with plain squared error: the same thing.

**7. Pick without peeking.** PySR returns its best formula at every length. The one kept has at
most ten added terms and scores best on the training split's own validation blocks. The test window
is read once, at the end.

**8. Check against the dumbest formula.** Cut the same search to one node and you get a constant:
ETAS times 1.5 everywhere (2.1 for L'Aquila). It keeps 61% of the network's gain on the tests. So
most of what the Field adds there is one fact: inside a big sequence, ETAS expects too few quakes.
The real formulas add 16 to 27 points on top, and the difference shows on the quiet training
years: there the constant does worse than ETAS, while the formulas still help.

**9. Read the law.** The shortest one, from L'Aquila's training years:

    rate = ETAS × exp( 1.18 × N(3 y) / N(12 y) )

N(3 y) and N(12 y) are the cell's quakes, each fading over 3 and 12 years. If a place's activity
is old, the ratio is near 0 and ETAS stands. Steady for 12 years: the ratio is 3/12 = 0.25, ×1.3.
All of it recent: ×2.6 (the Field's bound stops it there). It asks one question: *is this place
waking up?* All six laws lower ETAS where the last 12 years were busy, and five raise it after
recent quakes nearby. A likely reason (a reading, not yet tested): ETAS's "background" is a map of
past quakes, and it can't tell real background from the slow tail of an old sequence (van der Elst
called these orphaned aftershocks), so it keeps forecasting quakes where a sequence is dying out.

### The LLM analogy

**The canaries are a contamination check**: before trusting a benchmark score, you search the
training data for the test set. **The shuffled control is a label-permutation test**: train on
scrambled labels, and any accuracy left over comes from somewhere other than what you think.
**Symbolic distillation is a probe taken to its limit**: instead of asking "is concept X linearly
readable from the activations", you ask for the whole function, in ten terms. Where it breaks: an
LLM's behaviour doesn't fit in ten terms. The Field is tiny, and its job is one number per cell.

  unchanged. `FieldForecaster.audited` moves the cut for ETAS and the Field together;
  `shuffled_answers` deals the answers.

## P9f — A crust nobody can see, and a meter that reads it

### TLDR

The physics planets know something no real catalogue does: the state of every fault patch. CB-33
asks a forecast to rank the cells by that hidden state on days it never saw. craton-mini ranks them
at 0.45 (rank correlation), above a quake-count floor (0.39), ETAS (0.35) and Coulomb rate-and-state
(0.25). A Field forced to forecast *only* through Dieterich's stress law, the "stress meter", lost
everywhere, even on planets built from that law. Not kept.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Seismicity as a stress meter.** Dieterich (1994): on a fault obeying rate-and-state friction,
the quake rate R relative to the background rate r tells you the stress state, s = ln(R / r).
A push raises s at once, then it relaxes over t_a; a stress shadow pushes it below zero.

**2. A planted truth.** The physics planet (P8a) is built from that law, so it can write s down for
every patch. Per 0.1° cell, the patches' state averaged the way the cell's quakes see it. Half the
cells sit at steady state (nothing to rank), so only cells at least 10% off are graded, every 7th day.

**3. Reading a forecast.** Through the same law: ln(forecast rate / the cell's long-run rate).
Ranks only, so any monotone reading scores the same.

**4. The meter.** Make the Field's only output one stress per cell per day; rates follow through
Dieterich's law, counted day by day. Two things it forces: one stress moves every magnitude alike,
and extra quakes use up a cell's faults (a deficit that lowers later rates and fades as ETAS's own
quakes reload the cell).

**5. Why it lost.** Most of the loss (0.22 of 0.29 bits on CB-02) is the using-up: the data does not
want busy cells to go quiet on a 5-8 year clock. On the planets, each patch has its own t_a and state;
a cell's meter has one of each. The one part that paid: the shared stress sharpened *where* a little.

### Glossary

- **State (rate-and-state):** ln(R / r), how far a patch's quake rate sits from its background.
- **Stress shadow:** a place a quake unloaded, where the rate falls below normal.
- **t_a:** the time a fault's rate takes to settle after a change, Aσ/τ̇.

## P9g — A paper from 2010, and a reservoir the quakes can't see

### TLDR

Two checks against the world outside the model. **First**, a published fix: INGV's daily ETAS for
Italy undershot L'Aquila 2009 because one parameter, α, is bigger inside a sequence (Lombardi &
Marzocchi 2010). ETAS that refits its productivity every day on the last month ties the Field on the
2016-17 sequence (+0.30 bits per quake both) and gets half its gain on L'Aquila (+0.59 against +1.16).
**Second**, a measured hidden state: Groningen's gas pressure, mapped yearly. A forecast from quakes
alone maps where the field *drained* (+0.45 with the total pressure drop) and ranks the cells against
where it *drains now* (−0.42). So do the real quakes (−0.21). That year's change isn't in them.

### The idea, step by step

**1. What α does.** In ETAS each quake of magnitude m has on average K·e^{α(m − mc)} direct
aftershocks. K sets how productive quakes are overall; α sets how much more a big one makes than a
small one. One unit of magnitude multiplies the count by e^α: ×3.7 for α = 1.3, ×7.4 for α = 2.0.

**2. The paper.** Lombardi and Marzocchi fitted ETAS on all of Italy (α 1.3, p 1.16, from M2.5) for
the CSEP daily forecasts. When L'Aquila struck in April 2009, the model's expected count fell behind
the real one in the first days. Their reading: α there was 1.5 to 2.0. A national fit averages over
places that behave differently.

**3. The floor.** Keep everything of the fitted ETAS (background map, Omori's c and p, the spatial
kernel) and, every midnight, refit only K and α on the box's quakes of the last 30 days, every earlier
quake acting as a parent. Fewer than 100 such quakes: keep ETAS's own. That's `etas_local_alpha`.

**4. Making it cheap.** Refitting two numbers on thousands of quakes every day sounds slow. It isn't,
because K and α only multiply each parent's contribution. Sum each quake's triggering by the parent's
magnitude once (on the GPU), and any (K, α) gives every rate as one dot product. Both sequences run in
under three minutes.

**5. What it finds.** On the 2016-17 sequence, the day-by-day gain of the floor and of the Field have
a correlation of 0.99, and they end at the same +0.30. The Field's whole gain there is one fact:
inside the sequence, quakes were more productive than ETAS's average says. On L'Aquila the floor gets
+0.59 and the Field +1.16; 0.15 of the difference is *where*, which the floor can't change at all.

**6. α alone.** Refitting α with K held at ETAS's value lands at α 1.49 (2016) and 1.68 (2009) on the
busiest days, inside the paper's range. But it gains less (+0.19 and +0.40) than refitting both: the
data wants every quake more productive, big and small, more than it wants a steeper α.

**7. A truth somebody measured.** Groningen's gas field, 3 km under the Netherlands, has a pressure
map for every year since 1957 from the operator's reservoir model. CB-30 used it as an input. CB-31
turns it around: forecast the quakes of 2017-2023 *without* it (the model answers through a view of
the data that refuses those files) and grade the forecast's map of each year against where the pressure
fell that year.

**8. Why the yearly drop, not the total.** Looking at the truth first: the total drop since 1957 is
almost the same map every year after 2017 (rank correlation 0.99 between years). A test on it would
only ask "where did the field drain", which ten years of quakes already show. The yearly drop moves:
production was cut from 2014, the drained centre stopped falling and began to refill, and the edges
kept draining. By 2022 the yearly map runs against the total one (−0.66).

**9. The result.** Every quake-only map ranks the cells backwards: craton-mini −0.42, ETAS −0.47, last
year's quakes −0.23. The decisive check is the quakes that really came, used as if they were a forecast:
−0.21. Against the total drop all are positive (ETAS +0.52, mini +0.45, real quakes +0.26). The quakes
stay where the field drained most.

**10. Why that makes sense.** In rate-and-state friction a fault's quake rate responds to a change in
loading over a time t_a = Aσ/τ̇, which for slowly loaded faults is years to decades. Groningen's
faults were loaded for fifty years; one year of a smaller drop barely moves them. The quakes carry
the history of loading, and the test asked for its present. Nothing that reads only quakes can pass;
the task stays frozen as the number to beat once another kind of data joins.

### The LLM analogy

**The local-alpha floor is a strong baseline that embarrasses a fancy model**, like finding that a
tuned n-gram model with a cache matches your transformer on one benchmark. The cache here is "refit on
the last month". Where the analogy holds: a model's gain is only interesting above the best simple
thing anyone published. Where it breaks: on L'Aquila the network still doubles the baseline, and in
*where*, which no cache gives.

**CB-31 is a probing study with an external label**: does some reading of the model line up with a
quantity measured outside it? The lesson every probing paper learns: first check the label is
recoverable from the inputs at all. Here it isn't, and the "oracle" (the real quakes) shows it.

### What the code does

- `craton/bench/local_alpha.py`: `target_sums` (the GPU pass), `fit_day` (two-number maximum
  likelihood per day), `EtasLocalAlpha` (the forecast, with per-day K and α in the triggering and the
  cascade factor). `refit_k=False` is the α-only ablation.
- `scripts/ablations/local_alpha.py` → `results/ablation/local_alpha.json`: three seeds, both tasks,
  day-by-day comparison with the Field.
- `craton/bench/pressure.py`: `build_truth` (yearly and total drop per covered cell), `BlindView`
  (refuses the pressure files, raises `HiddenInstrumentError`), `GroningenPressure` (CB-31).
  craton-mini answers through `quake_only_forecaster`, its CB-30 Field trained without the push.
- `scripts/ablations/pressure_reference.py` → `results/ablation/pressure_reference.json`: the real
  quakes graded as a forecast.
- Figures: `scripts/figures/local_alpha.py`, `scripts/figures/pressure.py`.

## P9h — A region it never read

### TLDR

Train the world Field four times, each time without one of its four regions, and forecast that
region against ETAS fitted on the region's own past. It wins in all four, on every sequence and every
seed: **+0.34** bits per quake on average. The best simple fix (ETAS refit on the last month) gets
+0.07. Then we tried stripping the units from every input so all regions look alike. It helped the
two regions that look like the reference, hurt the two that don't, and lost on the mean (+0.30).

### The idea, step by step

**1. The question.** A model trained on California and tested on California might have memorised
California. The useful claim is that it learned how aftershock sequences behave anywhere. Hydrology
has a name and a test for this, prediction in ungauged basins: train on many rivers, forecast one
the model never saw (Kratzert et al. 2019 did it with LSTMs over 531 basins).

**2. CB-32.** Four folds. Fold "Japan" trains the world Field on central Italy, California and New
Zealand only, with the same cut (2007), network and members as craton-mini's, then forecasts Tohoku
2011 and Kumamoto 2016. The reference is ETAS fitted on the Japanese box's own past, so ETAS has seen
the region and the network hasn't. The score is the gain over that ETAS, averaged per region, then
over the four.

**3. Result.** +0.34. California +0.47, Japan +0.40, New Zealand +0.17, central Italy +0.30. When the
same regions are in training (CB-22) California scores +0.41, Japan +0.50, New Zealand +0.15. Leaving
a region out costs almost nothing.

**4. Central Italy is the exception.** The Italy-only Field gets +1.16 on L'Aquila; the fold that never
read Italy gets +0.41, and the floor that refits ETAS's productivity on the last month gets +0.59.
Italy's own past is worth something there.

**5. Why it transfers at all.** The world Field's inputs were already partly relative: magnitudes as
m − mc, and every output is a bounded nudge on top of ETAS fitted on the box. ETAS absorbs most of
what differs between regions (how many quakes, how fast they decay). The network only learns the
correction, and the correction looks alike everywhere.

**6. Going further: no units at all.** If relative inputs transfer, fully dimensionless ones should
transfer better. Three changes: each counter over what ETAS expects it to hold (a ratio, so ten times
the quakes changes nothing); counter lifetimes measured in units of each box's Omori c (the minutes
after a quake when the decay hasn't started), done by sliding along the log-spaced bank of
lifetimes; and one more spread, each quake counted over a Gaussian as wide as half its rupture
(Wells & Coppersmith 1994: log10 L = −2.44 + 0.59 M, so an M6 breaks ~13 km, an M7 ~50 km).

**7. v1 and v2.** v1 on one seed beat the world Field on the mean, but lost Tohoku badly: it
expected too few quakes. Reading that was reading a test sequence, so we allowed one change and then
froze: v2 adds the box's ETAS rate over its own long-run rate, a unitless "how deep into a sequence
are we". Tohoku stayed lost.

**8. Three seeds.** v2 against the world Field, paired by seed: CB-32 −0.03, CB-22 −0.02. Underneath,
a clean split: central Italy +0.19 and California +0.11 on every seed, Japan −0.28 and New Zealand
−0.14 on every seed. The reference Omori clock is Italy's. The ratios remove what the network was
using to tell the regions apart.

**9. Verdict.** Not kept. A v3 tuned on these numbers would be fitted to the test.

### The LLM analogy

**CB-32 is a held-out-domain evaluation**: train a language model on news, books and code, test on
legal text it never saw. The result says the model learned something general about sequences of
aftershocks. Where it breaks: an LLM's domains share one vocabulary; here ETAS, fitted on the new
region, does the translating before the network sees anything.

**Dimensionless inputs are aggressive normalisation**, like lower-casing and stripping every number
from text before training. It can help transfer, and it throws away signal the model was quietly
using. Normalise only what you know is noise.

### What the code does

- `craton/bench/unseen.py`: `UnseenRegions` (CB-32), `FOLDS`, the per-sequence and per-region gains.
- `craton/field/world.py`: `WorldField.holdout` (the fold's pool drops the region's tiles),
  `dimless_inputs` (the rupture sidecar and the Omori slide per tile).
- `craton/field/dimless.py`: `rupture_km`, `footprint`, `rupture_memory`, `omori_shift`,
  `DimlessFieldNet.features` (ratios, shares and the v2 anchor).
- `scripts/ablations/unseen_regions.py` trains the folds; `scripts/ablations/dimless.py` scores both

## P9i — How quakes slipped, and the tests a map has to pass

### TLDR

A quake's moment tensor says how it slipped, and the way it slipped points along the squeeze. Craton
now reads the Global CMT catalogue (every big quake since 1976, anywhere) and answers a new exam,
CB-34: which way is the crust squeezed, graded only on measurements nobody took from a quake
(boreholes, drilling, hydraulic fractures). Craton's tensor field gets **31.3°** off. The textbook
recipe from the same mechanisms gets 33.4°, and copying the neighbouring boreholes' own answers 27.9°.
A new referee for maps checks every map in this README. Three checks on the Field found that each
Italian forecast score rests on one day, and that the Field's ×4 cap is pinned on the L'Aquila night.

### The idea, step by step

**1. Why positions weren't enough.** CB-23 (P9a) asked for the squeeze from where quakes happen. A
line of quakes along a normal fault runs *along* the squeeze; along a thrust it runs *across* it.
Positions can't tell which, so every quake-only method sat near 45°, a coin.

**2. The moment tensor.** Seismologists fit each big quake's waves with six numbers: a symmetric
3×3 tensor. Its most negative direction is the **pressure axis**, roughly the squeeze. The Global CMT
project has computed one for nearly every quake of about magnitude 5 and up since 1976: 56,832 of them.
They become tokens of a new instrument, `moment_tensor` (Token Spec 1.3.0: added, nothing changed).

**3. A fair exam.** The World Stress Map is mostly made of focal mechanisms. Grading a mechanism
reader on mechanisms would grade the input against itself. So CB-34 keeps only the stress records
nobody read from a quake: borehole breakouts (the hole goes oval across the squeeze), drilling
fractures, hydraulic fracturing, overcoring, fault scratches. They are rare: 190 half-degree cells in
seven regions.

**4. The textbook floor.** Kostrov (1974): add up the tensors near a place, each scaled to the same
size, so one big quake can't outvote fifty small ones. Then take the horizontal direction where the
sum squeezes most (Lund & Townend 2007 showed that's the right definition when no stress axis is
vertical). No learning. 33.4°.

**5. The tensor field.** Masked modelling, the same game as the trunk's: hide one mechanism and
predict its shape from its 48 nearest neighbours (where they are, how deep, how big, how long ago,
how they slipped). Three rules keep it honest. The hidden quake's own aftershocks are hidden too (a
borehole has no twin to copy). Every example is spun about the vertical by a random angle, so the
network can't memorise "Japan squeezes east-west" and has to read its neighbours. And it never sees
Turkey, Chile or Alaska, or any stress record at all.

**6. Did it learn anything?** On mechanisms of 2003-06 it never trained on, the field's shapes miss by
0.298 (0 = perfect, 1 = unrelated); Kostrov's sum misses by 0.313, the nearest neighbour by 0.399. So
it does more than average. On CB-34: 31.3°, with Chile 26° against Kostrov's 38° (a region it never
saw). It does not beat copying the neighbouring cells' own boreholes: that reads the answers.

**7. A referee for maps.** In P7 a tidal "discovery" died when a 24-hour clock matched just as well.
Maps fool the same way: any smooth map agrees a little with any other, and a map that knows only the
regional average looks right wherever the average is. So a map claim has to beat a coin, beat its
floor, beat 95% of 200 copies of itself **slid around the region** (same texture, alignment broken),
and keep its lead on half the cells after the other half's lead was written down and hashed.

**8. What the referee said.** CB-23's map passes. CB-34's "beats Kostrov" passes on all three seeds.
CB-33's "beats quake density" passes on planets 0 and 2 and fails the locked half on planet 1, so its
README text now says two of three. CB-31's Groningen map fails everything, which is what the README
already said.

**9. One day each.** Score CB-02 day by day and drop 30 October 2016 (Norcia, a quarter of the
quakes): craton-mini falls to −0.02 bits per quake against ETAS. Its whole Apennine gain is that day.
Drop 6 April 2009 from CB-03: craton-mini keeps +0.89, but its lead over ETAS-refit-daily shrinks from
+0.58 to +0.09.

**10. The cap.** The Field may move ETAS at most ×4 in a cell. On the L'Aquila test days 82% of the
quakes landed in a cell-day where some member sat at the cap; on the mainshock day all six did. Official
forecasts that day were 5-25× above normal. A Field allowed ×16 won that night
(+0.18 bits per quake of the whole task) and lost more on every other day (−0.29): with room to
multiply by 16 it overshoots the days after. The cap is a brake. Knowing which night is the big one
is the next phase's question, not a bigger number.

**11. A linear probe.** Hydrology LSTMs turned out to store snow and soil moisture in their cells,
readable with a straight line. On the physics planets, a straight line reads the true fault state from
the Field's hidden features at 0.36, from its plain inputs at 0.38, and the forecast itself (no fitting)
gets 0.45. At this size, the inside holds nothing the forecast doesn't already say.

### The LLM analogy

**The tensor field is BERT for mechanisms**: mask a token, predict it from context. The random spin is
data augmentation that bakes in a symmetry, like training a vision model on rotated images so it
can't lean on "up is up". Where it breaks: the "context" here is a neighbourhood in space, not a
sentence, and the answer is a tensor, scored by how well it lines up.

**The map referee is a contamination and robustness check** in one: shifted maps are the "same answer
on a shuffled benchmark" control; the locked half is a pre-registered split.

**The linear probe** is exactly what interpretability people do to language models (does layer 6
"know" the part of speech?). A negative probe doesn't prove the information isn't there; it says it
isn't there in a straight line.

### Check yourself

1. Why can't CB-34 grade on all World Stress Map records?
   *(85% of them are focal mechanisms, many from the same catalogue the method reads. It would be
   graded on its own input.)*
2. What does the random spin about the vertical stop the tensor field from doing?
   *(Learning a fixed direction per place. Spun, "east-west" means nothing; only the neighbours'
   slip, spun with it, does.)*
3. A map scores well, and just as well when slid 200 km sideways. What has it learned?
   *(The regional average, or a texture: nothing about where the truth's features are.)*
4. Why does "the whole CB-02 gain is one day" matter?
   *(One day is one draw. A gain that lives on one day says little about the next sequence.)*

### Glossary

- **Moment tensor:** six numbers describing how a quake's two sides moved; its pressure axis points
  roughly along the squeeze.
- **Kostrov sum:** adding many quakes' tensors to get the average deformation of a region.
- **SHmax:** the direction of the largest horizontal compressive stress.
- **Borehole breakout:** the well wall spalls off on the sides facing the weakest push, so the hole goes
  oval across SHmax.
- **Toroidal shift:** sliding a map across itself with wrap-around, to keep its texture and break its
  position.
- **Linear probe:** a straight-line fit from a network's internal features to a known quantity.
