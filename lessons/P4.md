# P4 — The first model: Craton-mini v0

## TLDR

Craton has a brain now: a 4.7-million-parameter transformer that reads the INGV earthquake catalogue.
It trained on a laptop GPU in 8 to 16 minutes per run and never used more than 2.6 GB of the
6 GB card.

It plays two games. Hide a quake and guess where it was and how big (CB-01), and stand at midnight and
forecast tomorrow (CB-02, CB-03). On the first it beats the floor: **21.66 bits** per hidden measurement
against climatology's 22.66. On the second it loses to ETAS, plainly: **−10.88 bits** per M3+ quake on
the 2016-17 Apennines against ETAS's −7.12, though it beats the flat Poisson rate (−14.64). That loss is
on the scoreboard, and P5 to P7 exist to close it.

## The idea, step by step

**1. A quake becomes a vector.** A language model looks each word up in a table and gets back a list of
numbers. A quake has no table entry; it has numbers already: latitude, longitude, depth, magnitude, plus
a label for the magnitude type (Md, ML, Mw). A small network (the *encoder*) maps those into 256 numbers,
the same width the rest of the model works in. That list is the quake's *embedding*.

**2. Position, in four dimensions.** Word 7 comes after word 6; that's all a sentence needs. A quake has
four coordinates: where on the map (two), how deep, and when. Craton uses *rotary* encoding (RoPE, the
one most modern LLMs use): inside every attention head, pairs of numbers get turned by an angle
proportional to a coordinate. When two quakes are compared, only the *difference* of their angles
survives. So the model sees "3 km apart and 2 hours later", never "at 42.7°N", and what it learns in
Norcia works the same in L'Aquila. Each head splits its 32 numbers into four blocks, one per axis, with
waves from a minute to four years for time, 2 to 200 km for the map, and 3 and 30 km for depth.

**3. A hidden coordinate gets no angle at all.** When the model has to guess where a hidden quake was, its
map coordinates mustn't leak in through the positional encoding. Its map blocks are set to zero instead
of turned, so it can only be matched by time and by content. A test changes a hidden quake's coordinates
by a degree and checks the model's output doesn't move by a single bit.

**4. Attention: every quake looks at every other.** A window of 1,024 consecutive quakes goes through six
layers. In each one, every quake asks which others are relevant (attention), takes a weighted average
of them, then thinks alone for a moment (a small feed-forward network). That's the GPT-2 block, unchanged.
The trunk knows nothing about geology. It only knows how to mix tokens.

**5. Game one: fill in the blank.** Hide 15% of the quakes in the window: keep each one's time and
magnitude type, hide where it was and how big. The model answers with a probability for every 0.01° of
latitude, every 0.01° of longitude, every kilometre of depth and every 0.1 of magnitude, the exact bins
CB-01 scores. The training loss *is* CB-01's score: bits on the true bin. That's BERT's game, played with
earthquakes.

**6. Game two: tomorrow.** Put a special *query* token at midnight, after the 1,023 quakes before it. Its
output answers three questions: how many quakes tomorrow (a Poisson rate), where (a probability for each
of the 1,050 cells of 0.1°), and how big (a probability for each 0.1 of magnitude). Multiply them and you
get the expected number of quakes in every cell and every magnitude bin, which is exactly the forecast
CB-02 grades. The loss is CB-02's own score again, the Poisson log-likelihood.

**7. Never a single number.** Both heads only output distributions. Each also keeps 0.1% of its
probability spread evenly over every bin, so no answer is ever "impossible" and no single surprise can
cost more than about 20 bits.

**8. The first version couldn't see a crisis.** The first forecasts for 2016 expected about 60 M3+ quakes
where 1,065 happened, no better than the flat Poisson rate. Two reasons. Attention *averages*, so it can't
count how crowded its window is: a window holding one quiet year and one holding one frantic afternoon
look alike after the average. And only 13 days in thirty years of training data had more than 200
quakes; 2016 was busier than all of them. A neural net doesn't extrapolate from 13 examples. Neither
would you, if your whole idea of "a bad day" came from 13 bad days.

**9. The fix: tell it the rate, in a straight line.** Each token now carries a clock (how long ago it
happened, how long since the quake before it), and the rate head gets a straight path from six measured
rates: "the last quake was 3 minutes ago", "the last 1,000 took 9 hours", "the last M3+ was 40 minutes
ago". A straight line in log space keeps going past anything it saw in training, which is the shape of
Omori's law. The model starts from "tomorrow looks like now" and learns the corrections. In the trial run
that proved the fix, it expected 579 M3+ quakes for 2016-17; ETAS expected 583, and 1,065 came. (The
final three seeds average 320; see the numbers.)

**10. Train on the past only.** Three separate models per seed. The CB-01 model reads the `pretrain`
split. The CB-02 model reads only data from before 24 August 2016, and the CB-03 model only data from
before 2009, so neither has ever seen how its test sequence ended. Nine training runs in total.

**11. Stop when it stops learning.** One 30-day block in ten is kept aside for validation. Every 100 steps
the loop scores it, keeps the best weights, and stops after three checks without progress. Small data
plus a big model overfits fast; early stopping is the cheapest guard.

## The LLM analogy

| LLM world | Craton-mini v0 |
|---|---|
| Token embedding table | the event encoder: four numbers and a label → 256 |
| RoPE over word positions | RoPE over (time, lat, lon, depth) |
| GPT-2 small, pre-norm blocks | the same block, 6 layers, d = 256 |
| BERT's masked-word objective | hide 15% of quakes, guess place and size |
| Next-token prediction | next-day prediction: rate × where × how big |
| Cross-entropy in bits | CB-01's bits; CB-02's Poisson log-likelihood |
| fp16 mixed precision + loss scaling | the same, on a Turing GPU (no bf16) |
| Warmup + cosine schedule, early stopping | the same |
| MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) | CB-15 |

**Where it breaks.** Words are discrete; quakes are continuous, so the "vocabulary" is a set of bins. A
sentence is ordered by position alone; a catalogue has four coordinates, and two quakes can share a
second. And an LLM has trillions of tokens; Craton-mini has about 300,000 quakes. By the Chinchilla rule
(about 20 tokens per parameter) a 4.7M model wants 100 million. That gap is why P7 builds fake planets.

## What the code does

In the order data flows:

1. `craton/train/data.py` cuts examples from a split: `MaskedWindows` (1,024 quakes, 15% hidden) and
   `NextDay` (1,023 quakes, then a query at midnight, plus the counts of the next 24 hours). Hidden values
   are zeroed right there; they never leave the function.
2. `craton/model/encoders.py` turns quakes into vectors, registered by instrument (P5 adds GNSS here).
3. `craton/model/positional.py` holds the 4D rotary encoding and the sinusoidal fallback.
4. `craton/model/trunk.py` is the transformer: attention + feed-forward, six times.
5. `craton/model/heads.py` holds the masked head, the next-day head and their losses in bits.
6. `craton/train/loop.py` is the training loop: fp16 autocast, gradient scaling, AdamW, warmup + cosine,
   validation, early stopping, peak VRAM and tokens per second.
7. `craton/train/checkpoint.py` names each checkpoint by everything that made it and writes its model card.
8. `craton/train/method.py` plugs the model into CratonBench as the method `craton-mini`.

## The numbers

Every number is a mean over seeds 0, 1 and 2, from `results/scoreboard.json`.

| Task | Craton-mini v0 | Climatology / Poisson | ETAS |
|---|---|---|---|
| CB-01 bits per hidden measurement (lower is better) | **21.66 ± 0.03** | 22.66 | · |
| CB-02 bits per M3+ quake, 2016-17 (higher is better) | **−10.88 ± 0.67** | −14.64 | −7.12 |
| CB-03 bits per M3+ quake, 2009 | **−15.13 ± 0.79** | −16.94 | −9.40 |
| CB-15 share of the GPU's peak used | **15.6% ± 0.1** | · | · |

**CB-01, inside the number.** The quakes alone went from 22.48 bits (climatology) to 20.49: two bits, a
factor of four in probability on the true answer. Latitude 7.42 → 6.89 bits, longitude 7.02 → 6.22,
magnitude 4.67 → 4.06, depth 3.37 → 3.33. Depth barely moved: the catalogue's depths cluster at a few
standard values, and climatology already knows them. The GNSS half is copied from climatology (v0 can't
read GPS yet), which is why the headline moved one bit, not two.

**CB-02.** Better than ignoring aftershocks by 3.8 bits per quake, worse than ETAS by 3.8. The three seeds
disagree a lot (−10.22, −10.85, −11.57): with 13 busy days to learn a crisis from, small changes in
initialisation decide what the model makes of them. On average it expected 320 M3+ quakes where 1,065
came; ETAS expected 583. At M1.5+ it scores −6.40 bits against ETAS's −2.76.

**CB-03.** The weakest result. Trained only on years before 2009, it has one big sequence to learn from
(Colfiorito, 1997, in the old Md magnitudes), and it overshoots: 495 M3+ quakes expected for 2009, where
315 happened. It still beats Poisson by 1.8 bits; it loses to ETAS by 5.7.

**Why ETAS still wins.** ETAS is a theory: Omori's decay, Gutenberg-Richter, triggering that scales with
magnitude, all fitted with a handful of parameters. Craton-mini is 5.5 million parameters with no theory,
trained on about 132,000 quakes and only 13 days with more than 200 of them. A transformer learns laws from
examples, and it hasn't seen enough examples of a crisis. That's the plan's bet for the next phases: GPS
and tides as extra eyes (P5), and simulated planets with thousands of sequences (P7).

**Cost.** The CB-01 run: 8 minutes, 85,000 tokens per second, 1.35 GB of VRAM peak. The forecast runs:
12 to 16 minutes, 2.6 GB peak. The whole benchmark, nine trainings plus scoring: 2 hours 4 minutes on the
laptop. Most runs were still improving when they hit the 2,500-step limit.

## Try it yourself

```console
$ uv run craton train cb03_fit --seeds 0      # one model, about 16 minutes on an RTX 2060
$ cat results/checkpoints/*cb03_fit*s0/card.md # its model card
$ uv run craton bench run craton-mini --tasks CB-03 --seeds 0
```

Then open `configs/mini.yaml`, set `positional: sinusoidal`, and train again: the fallback encoding, with
absolute positions instead of differences. (It's a different config, so it gets its own checkpoint.)

## Check yourself

<details><summary>Why does a hidden quake get zeroed rotary blocks instead of its true angles?</summary>

Its angles *are* its coordinates. Turning its features by them would hand the answer to every other
token it's compared with. Zeroed blocks mean it can only be matched by time and content.
</details>

<details><summary>The masked loss and CB-01 are the same number. Why is that a good idea, and when isn't it?</summary>

The model is trained on exactly what it's judged by, so nothing is lost in translation. The risk is
the usual one: optimise a test hard enough and you learn its quirks. That's why CB-01's held-out eras
never enter training, and why validation blocks decide when to stop.
</details>

<details><summary>Why can't attention just count how many quakes happened yesterday?</summary>

Attention's weights sum to one. It returns a weighted *average*, which looks the same whether it
averaged 10 quakes or 1,000. Counts have to arrive as inputs.
</details>

<details><summary>Why one model per task split instead of one model for everything?</summary>

The `pretrain` split contains 2018 to 2026, the tail of the 2016 sequence. A CB-02 forecaster trained on
it would have seen how the aftershocks ended: a leak by time, even though no test token is in it.
</details>

<details><summary>Why is CB-15 a utilisation percentage, not memory or speed?</summary>

The ratchet never lets a task get worse. Score memory or speed, and the first bigger model would be
blocked forever. Utilisation doesn't fall just because a model grows; the 6 GB limit is a hard gate.
</details>

## Glossary

- **Embedding**: the list of numbers a token becomes inside the model.
- **Rotary encoding (RoPE)**: position given by turning pairs of numbers, so attention sees differences.
- **Pre-norm block**: layer norm *before* attention and feed-forward; trains more stably when deep.
- **Masked modelling**: hide part of the input, train the model to fill it in.
- **Binned head**: a probability for each bin of a continuous value.
- **Mixed precision (fp16)**: half-size numbers for speed; a *gradient scaler* stops tiny gradients from
  rounding to zero.
- **Warmup + cosine**: the learning rate climbs, then glides down along half a cosine.
- **Early stopping**: keep the weights from the best validation check, stop when checks stop improving.
- **MFU**: the share of the GPU's peak arithmetic a training run actually used.
- **Model card**: a page that says what a checkpoint is, what it was trained on, and what it's not for.
