# P6 — Beat ETAS: the Field

## TLDR

For two phases Craton lost to ETAS, the 1988 aftershock model every seismologist uses, by four to five
bits per quake. P6 found out why: the forecast forgot (a window of 1,024 quakes reached back less than
three days in the middle of a sequence) and squeezed a map of 1,050 places out of one vector.

So the forecast was rebuilt as **the Field**: every 0.1° cell of central Italy is a token with a fading
memory of the quakes in it, the cells look at each other with attention, and the whole thing *starts as
ETAS* and learns only a bounded correction.

Craton is now **above ETAS on both forecasts**: CB-02 (Apennines 2016-17) **−6.82** against ETAS
−7.12, CB-03 (L'Aquila 2009) **−8.23** against −9.40. Almost all of it is *how many* quakes come.
*Where* they land: better than ETAS on L'Aquila, a hair worse on the Apennines, and every bit of that
loss sits on the two days a new mainshock struck somewhere else.

## The idea, step by step

**1. Split the loss before fixing it.** A forecast's score per quake is a sum of exactly four parts:
how many quakes the day had (a Poisson total), where each one was (the share of the day's rate in its
cell), how big (the share of the cell's rate in its magnitude bin), and a shuffle term that is the
same for every forecast. P5 was 4.7 bits behind ETAS on CB-02: 3.6 of them were *where*. Every
forecast score now comes with this split.

**2. Measure the room.** How far above ETAS could anyone get? Give ETAS the true count of each day:
+2.1 bits. Give it the true map, to the exact cell: +2.1. Both together, the roof: +4.2 on CB-02,
+6.0 on CB-03. These oracles read the answers, so nobody can reach them; they show where the room is
(most of the count room sits on the three days with an M5+ quake). That is the building on the
scoreboard.

**3. Omori as a sum of fading counters.** ETAS says a quake's aftershocks fade like (t + c)^−p, a power
law. A power law has no favourite timescale, and you can build one from exponentials with
lifetimes spread over many decades: an hour, four hours, a day, four days, … twelve years. So each
cell keeps nine counters. A quake adds to them; each counter forgets e^−Δt/τ of itself as time passes.
ETAS's whole memory of the past is then one fixed recipe over the counters, and a network can learn
other recipes. The counters count every quake down to M1.5, once plainly and once weighted by
10^(m−2) (bigger quakes make exponentially more aftershocks), in the cell itself and in blurred
neighbourhoods of ~10 and ~30 km. 54 numbers per cell per day, at the same cost however long the
history.

**4. Cells as tokens.** At midnight, each of the 1,050 cells becomes a token: its memory, ETAS's
rate for it, the whole region's ETAS rate, and where it is. Two attention layers let each cell read
the others, the way a vision transformer reads image patches. Without that attention, *where* got
0.36 bits worse on CB-03: a cell needs its neighbours to know where a sequence is going.

**5. Start from ETAS, learn the difference.** Out of each cell come two numbers: how much to move
ETAS's rate of M2-3 quakes there, and of M3+ ones. The output layer starts at zero, so the untrained
Field *is* ETAS. The correction is bounded: at most 4x either way. The network only has to learn
what ETAS gets wrong, which is a much smaller job than learning seismology from scratch.

**6. Small quakes in, big quakes scored.** The memory reads M1.5+, the loss learns from M2+ (three
times more examples than M3+), and only the M3+ output is scored. The literature says small quakes
are the main thing ETAS leaves on the table (Mizrahi et al. 2021), and the bit split agrees.

**7. Four Fields, averaged.** One Field per seed swung CB-02 between −6.87 and −7.10. Averaging four
of them (a deep ensemble) brought the spread down to ±0.02: they disagree where training data is
thin, and their average is a better forecast than any one of them.

**8. More sequences.** The CB-02 Field first learned from 2005 on, where one big sequence lives
(L'Aquila 2009). Starting in 1991 adds Colfiorito 1997, in the older Md magnitudes, with ETAS (fitted
from 2005) still as the starting line. That alone took CB-03 from −8.61 to −8.25 on seed 0 and
made its *where* beat ETAS.

**9. Two stronger floors, and what they say.** ETAS with stretched aftershock zones (ellipses along
the first hours of aftershocks): zero gain. Its M6 kernel is ~3 km wide, and the thousands of small
aftershocks, each a parent of its own, already draw the fault. ETAS that knows the network goes blind
after big quakes (Hainzl's blind time): the daily counts choose a blind time of zero, and the small
gain comes from scaling ETAS's productivity up by 10%. Both are on the board; the Field beats them.

## The LLM analogy

- **State-space layers (S4, Mamba).** The memory is a diagonal state-space layer with fixed decays:
  a running state per cell, updated once a day, that remembers the whole past at constant cost.
  Where it breaks: Mamba learns its decays; here they are fixed and log-spaced, because the physics
  (a power law) says which ones are needed.
- **Vision transformers.** The map is a grid of patches, and attention between them is how a cell
  learns from its neighbourhood.
- **Residual learning / fine-tuning.** The Field starts from a model that already works and learns a
  bounded difference, the same reason fine-tuning a pretrained LLM beats training one from zero.
- **Deep ensembles.** Four members, averaged.

## What the code does

1. `craton/bench/tasks.py` → `bit_split`: the four-part split, as extras of CB-02 and CB-03.
2. `craton/bench/ceiling.py`: the oracles; `craton bench ceiling` writes `results/ceiling.json`.
3. `craton/bench/baselines.py`: `interpolation` (CB-01's honest GNSS floor), `etas_aniso`,
   `etas_incomplete`.
4. `craton/field/memory.py`: the fading counters.
5. `craton/field/base.py`: ETAS's daily rates on the GPU, for thousands of training days.
6. `craton/field/model.py`: `FieldNet`, cells as tokens, zero-initialised output, bounded nudge.
7. `craton/field/forecaster.py`: build the days, train four members, cache checkpoints, forecast.
8. `craton/train/method.py`: `craton-mini` now forecasts through the Field; CB-01, CB-04 and CB-17
   still come from the P5 trunk.

## The numbers

Mean of 3 seeds, each the average of four Fields. Bits per M3+ quake, higher is better.

| | CB-02 Apennines 2016-17 | CB-03 L'Aquila 2009 |
|---|---|---|
| Craton P5 | −10.98 | −14.05 |
| ETAS | −7.12 | −9.40 |
| ETAS-incomplete (strongest floor) | −7.06 | −9.30 |
| **Craton P6, the Field** | **−6.82 ± 0.02** | **−8.23 ± 0.01** |
| … above ETAS | +0.30 (1.2x its probability) | +1.16 (2.2x) |
| how many, vs ETAS | +0.32 | +1.02 |
| where, vs ETAS | −0.02 | +0.15 |
| without the M5+ days, vs ETAS | −0.09 | +0.70 |
| the roof (oracles) | +4.21 | +6.00 |

What it means in plain words:

- **The win is real and steady.** Four averaged Fields per seed, and the three seeds agree to a few
  hundredths of a bit; ETAS loses beyond any noise on both sequences.
- **It is mostly counting.** The Field knows better how many quakes tomorrow brings, from its memory of
  small quakes and from having seen three sequences (Colfiorito 1997, L'Aquila 2009, and the quiet years
  between) instead of none.
- **Where is a tie on the Apennines.** Better than ETAS on every day except Norcia (30 October 2016) and
  Campotosto (18 January 2017), when a new mainshock struck away from the swarm. That's the one exit test
  this phase misses, by 0.02 bits, and it's the mainshock days that the storm detector is for.
- **On calm Apennine days ETAS is still a bit better (−0.09).** The Field wins CB-02 on the busy days.
  On L'Aquila it wins on calm days too.
- **M1.5+:** CB-02 −2.95 against ETAS −2.76. The Field forecasts M3+ and extends down by
  Gutenberg-Richter; its M2-3 output isn't used for small quakes yet.
- **Everything else is untouched**: CB-01 15.12 (quakes 20.48, GNSS 9.75), CB-04 17.50 mm, CB-15
  16.27%, CB-17 24.56, the same trunk checkpoints as P5.
- **A new honest bar:** drawing a line between a GNSS station's neighbouring days scores 9.65 bits on
  CB-01's GNSS half, a tenth of a bit better than Craton. Craton's GNSS skill so far is interpolation.

## Check yourself

<details><summary>1. Why can nine exponentials stand in for a power law?</summary>

A power law has the same shape at every timescale. A sum of exponentials whose lifetimes are spread
evenly in log-time (one per factor of four) has roughly the same "no favourite scale" property; with
the right weights it traces (t + c)^−p over the whole range they span.
</details>

<details><summary>2. Why does the output layer start at zero?</summary>

Because then the untrained Field forecasts exactly what ETAS does, and training can only move it where
the data says ETAS is wrong. Starting anywhere else would mean first unlearning noise.
</details>

<details><summary>3. The Field's map is worse than ETAS's on exactly two days. Why those two?</summary>

30 October 2016 (Norcia, M6.5) and 18 January 2017 (Campotosto). On both, a new mainshock struck away
from where the recent quakes were. The Field leans harder on recent activity than ETAS, so when the
next big one is somewhere else, it pays more. Seeing a mainshock coming is the storm detector's job
(P11), not the Field's.
</details>

<details><summary>4. Stretched aftershock zones are physically right. Why do they add nothing?</summary>

Because ETAS already gets the shape from the aftershocks themselves: every small aftershock is a parent
with its own small zone, and thousands of them lie along the fault. The ellipse of one big parent
changes a sliver of the total rate.
</details>

## Glossary

- **Bit split**: a forecast's score per quake as how-many + where + how-big + shuffle.
- **Oracle / the building**: ETAS with part of the truth put in; it measures the room above ETAS.
- **Omori memory**: fading counters per cell whose weighted sum can trace any Omori-like decay.
- **Cell tokens**: the forecast grid's cells as the tokens attention runs over.
- **Bounded nudge**: the most the Field may move ETAS, here 4x either way.
- **Deep ensemble**: several networks trained from different seeds, their forecasts averaged.
- **Blind time**: how long a network stays blind to small quakes after a bigger one.
